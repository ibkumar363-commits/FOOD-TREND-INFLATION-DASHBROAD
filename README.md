Food Price Trends & Inflation
Selected Food Prices Nigeria, November 2024 to April 2025
Prepared from the Selected Food Price Watch dataset, standardized across a Jan-2025 item list change.
1. National Price Trend
Summing the 33 items tracked continuously across all six months, the total basket rose from ₦84,423 in November 2024 to ₦95,788 in April 2025 a 13.5% increase over five months, roughly 2.5% per month compounded.
The rise wasn't a straight line. Prices climbed steadily through March, then eased slightly in April (₦97,158 → ₦95,788) the only month-over-month dip in the entire window.
Month	Basket Total (₦)
Nov-2024	84,423
Dec-2024	85,881
Jan-2025	89,710
Feb-2025	91,022
Mar-2025	97,158
Apr-2025	95,788

2. Inflation Is Not Evenly Spread Across Food Groups
Average year-on-year (YoY) inflation varies sharply by category as of April 2025:
Category	Avg YoY %
Staples (rice, beans, garri, yam, plantain)	99.7%
Fish / seafood	87.5%
Processed / dairy	74.4%
Meat / poultry / eggs	73.8%
Cooking oils	71.9%
Vegetables / fruit	62.1%

Staples effectively doubled year-on-year. This is the most consequential finding in the dataset. Staples form the base of most Nigerian households' calorie intake and are the least substitutable food group, so this is where inflation is felt hardest particularly by low-income households.
3. A Small Set of Items Is Driving Volatility
Local Rice (Broken), Beans (Brown & White), Plantain, and Yam Tuber lead YoY inflation at 114–158%, all staples, consistent with the finding above.
Separately, Onions and Agric Hen Eggs (crate of 30) are the most volatile items month-to-month (standard deviation of MoM change of roughly 27% and 17% respectively). These swing sharply rather than trending steadily a supply-chain and perishability signature rather than a structural inflation one.
4. Geography Matters More Than Expected
The average gap between a food's highest priced state and its lowest-priced state is 123% the median gap is exactly 100%, meaning for a typical item in a typical month, the price simply doubles between the cheapest and most expensive state.
Item	Avg State Price Gap
Onions, fresh	302%
Agric Hen Eggs (crate of 30)	267%
Sweet potatoes	259%
Mudfish, dried	247%
Tomatoes, fresh	209%

Northeastern states (Yobe, Borno, Adamawa, Taraba, Gombe, Bauchi) appear as the lowest-price state 91 times across the dataset, versus only 34 times as the highest-price state. This is consistent with these being production zones where the crop is grown, but transport and insecurity constraints limit onward distribution — the price gap reflects logistics friction more than production cost.
5. Recommendations
On the April price dip
Treat the April easing with caution in any report. One month is not a trend reversal, and it follows five consecutive months of increases. It should be described as “stabilizing” rather than “improving” until May/June data confirms the direction.
On food security framing
The staples finding, not the volatile perishables, is the headline. A roughly 100% YoY increase in rice, beans, and yam affects nutrition security more than a volatile onion price, because households can skip onions for a week but not staples. Any policy-facing summary of this data should lead with the staples figure.
On the state price gap
This reads as a logistics and storage problem rather than a scarcity one. Northeastern states are cheap sources but rarely the expensive destination, meaning value is being lost or captured elsewhere between farm-gate and market. A useful follow-up would be to check whether transport-cost or post-harvest-loss data exists for the northeastern corridor to confirm the mechanism, particularly using field-level data collection tools such as ODK or KoboCollect.
On using this dataset going forward
Ten items were discontinued and nine new items were introduced starting January 2025, so any YoY comparison beyond January 2025 is only fully reliable for the 27 items confirmed as continuous series. Any external facing use of this data should footnote that MoM/YoY figures for the remaining items may partly reflect a unit or brand change rather than pure price movement.

Source: Selected Food Price Watch, standardized dataset, Nov 2024–Apr 2025.
